## Why the "giant rulebook" idea is wrong

Why does AI sometimes feel like a giant, fixed rulebook? This idea is widespread, but it doesn't hold up in reality. Even in the context of the fierce rivalry between companies like OpenAI and Anthropic, and figures like Dario Amodei, this myth isn't backed by some static set of rules — it's backed by a much more complex technical reality. A classic program really does work the way its name suggests: a human writes down in advance exactly what should happen. If condition A holds, step B happens. If condition C applies, step D follows. The computer simply executes this logical chain without any particular effort — it just follows the strict instructions it was given.

With an AI model, things work differently. It isn't a collection of rigid rules that a programmer painstakingly typed in one by one. Instead, it's a mathematical structure made up of an enormous number of adjustable parameters. Parameters are the tunable numeric values that steer the model's behavior. You can picture them like the tiny dials on a watch, each of which can be nudged slightly.

So how exactly does the model manage to produce a sensible answer to a new input? Because during what's called training, it detected patterns in vast amounts of data. Rather than looking at individual facts, the system looks at the statistical relationships between many different words or images. That's how it learns which words or characters are likely to follow one another. Having internalized these patterns, it can make inferences about entirely new inputs without anyone ever having to give it an explicit rule for that specific case.

An important point here: the model doesn't "know" anything in the human sense. It calculates probabilities. When you ask a question, your text runs through a complex computation inside this mathematical structure. The parameters fixed during training don't change permanently while the model is in use — they simply serve as the fixed basis for the calculation that shapes the next likely answer. That's the actual mechanism behind what you'd call "intelligence."

You might now be wondering whether there isn't, somewhere, a list of "if this, then that" rules that the system works through. The answer is no. There is no fixed rulebook in the classic sense. The model is instead a flexible tool that behaves dynamically through the computational processing of its input.

This distinction matters because it dismantles the myth of the giant rulebook. An AI model isn't a book of fixed laws you look things up in. It's a system that has learned from experience — that is, from data — how language or images are structured. It uses its mathematical structure to work out the best possible continuation, based on the patterns it internalized during training.

## From fixed recipe to learning structure

When you make a cup of tea, you have a clear picture in your head: heat the water, add the teabag, wait, take it out. Each of these steps follows a fixed rule. If you make the water too hot, the tea turns bitter because you exceeded the temperature. The cause is clear, and the effect is predictable. That's how a classic computer program works. A human writes down exactly what the machine should do: "If condition A occurs, execute step B." The logic is prescribed from the outside. It's a rigid plan that runs the same way every single time. An AI model, however, works fundamentally differently. There's no fixed plan laid out in advance. Instead, the system looks at a huge number of examples — say, millions of texts or images. Rather than searching for fixed rules that a human wrote, it independently learns what patterns lie hidden in the data. So the logic isn't prescribed, it's derived from the data. That's the key difference: when programming, you determine the "why" and the "how"; when training, you let the system be guided by the "what." How exactly does this learning happen in detail? Why does this difference matter so much? Because a program only ever does exactly what it was told. If a rule is missing, it can't improvise. A learning model, on the other hand, develops a flexible structure. It can respond to situations that never appeared in its training data, because it has understood the general relationships instead of memorizing individual cases. You've probably noticed yourself how different this feels. If you explain to a friend how to order a pizza, you use fixed steps: call, order, pick up. But if you ask them to pick the "best" movie for the evening, you can't hand them a fixed list. You'd say: "Check the ratings, read the comments, and weigh the genre against your mood." They use their own judgment, built from experience. That's exactly what AI does too. It weighs things up instead of working through a list. This kind of learning makes AI models powerful, but also vulnerable. Because the logic comes from data, flawed or manipulated data can distort the model's behavior. A classic program would either throw an error on a faulty input value or rigidly execute its next rule regardless. An AI model, on the other hand, might interpret corrupted information as a new "truth," because it draws its structure from context. The quality of the output depends directly on the quality of the data. It also determines how the logic is anchored within the model. In summary: a program is like a recipe book you follow to the letter. An AI model is like an experienced cook who tastes the ingredients, senses the temperature, and creates a dish that isn't in any recipe book. One approach is reliable and controllable, the other is flexible and adaptive. Both have their place, but they solve problems in completely different ways.

## How the model "learns": patterns instead of instructions

Think back to the last time you explained to someone how to make a cup of coffee. You probably didn't write down mathematical formulas or complicated "if this, then that" rules. You more likely said: "Add water, switch the machine on, wait until it's brewed, then fill the cup." Those are concrete instructions. You tell the device exactly which steps to carry out, in which order, to produce a specific result.

Artificial intelligence, however, often works completely differently. A program controlled this way strictly follows a predetermined plan. If a situation arises that the plan didn't account for, it either stalls or produces a wrong result. It can't react "on instinct," because it only knows the instructions it was given.

So how exactly does the learning happen in detail? This is where machine learning comes in. Instead of giving a system an endless list of instructions, you give it lots of examples. You show the model tens of thousands of photos of dogs and cats, for instance. But you don't tell the system: "If you see four legs and a tail, it's an animal" or "If the head is pointy, it's a cat." Instead, you let the model guess for itself.

At first, the model guesses what's in the picture. Sometimes it's right, sometimes it's off. You then correct it. This cycle — guess, correct, guess, correct — repeats millions of times. Each correction causes the model to slightly adjust its internal settings, its parameters, to reduce the error.

Because the model sees so many examples, it starts to pick up on the underlying patterns. It notices that cats often have a certain ear shape, while dogs share other features. These insights get baked into the parameters. When the model later sees a new photo it's never encountered before, it draws on these stored patterns to make an educated guess. It's not following a fixed rule, but a probability derived from experience with all those examples.

You might pause here and consider: what would be the biggest advantage of a system finding patterns on its own, instead of us having to prepare it for every single situation?

That's exactly where the crucial difference lies. When we write instructions, we have to anticipate every possible exception. That quickly becomes unmanageable once the world gets complex. When a model learns patterns, it can also handle new situations, as long as they resemble the patterns it has recognized. It's more adaptable, because it doesn't rely on rigid rules but on the probabilities it has drawn from the data.

The technical term for this process is "training." It's important to understand that the model doesn't formulate logical statements while doing this. It merely optimizes the numbers in its parameters so that the gap between its guess and the correct answer becomes as small as possible. By the end of training, the model has stored a kind of compressed knowledge about the relationships within the examples. It hasn't learned the way a human learns, with language and concepts. It has learned which combination of numbers produces the best predictions for the data it was shown.

This is what fundamentally sets a model apart from a classic program. The program does exactly what it's told. The model does what it has inferred from the examples. It's less an executor of commands and more a system that reacts based on probabilities. That makes it powerful, but hard to understand. The model's "thoughts" aren't sentences — they're billions of tiny numerical adjustments.
