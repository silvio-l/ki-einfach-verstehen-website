## The vending machine: your first AI model

Think about your next cup of coffee: what actually happens when you type into a modern assistant's chat window and then get a text back?

To understand this, let's look at the ordinary vending machine in the break room. You press a particular button – say, for a coffee with milk. That's your input, technically called just that: input. The machine takes in this signal, processes it internally, and finally dispenses the drink you wanted. That result is the output. So you made an input, the machine did something with it, and you received a result. That's the basic principle behind any technical processing: a given input leads to a given output.

The basic principle is clear. Now let's look inside the black box. When you ask a question, that's your input. The model takes in this information, runs it through an internal computation process, and then gives you an answer. That's the output. You can picture it like a huge black box: you put something in on one side, and something different comes out the other. Except with an AI model, it's not simply applying a fixed, hard-coded rule like "press button A -> coffee." Instead, the model worked out its own rules during a long training phase, so it can generate meaningful outputs from arbitrary inputs.

Here's a good place to pause and guess for yourself: what's the crucial difference between the machine and the AI model when it comes to the kind of input each one accepts?

With the vending machine, the range is very narrow. You can only press the buttons it offers. If you press the coffee button, you always get coffee. If you try to explain to the machine that you actually just want water today, even though you're pressing the coffee button, it won't understand you. It only knows its fixed, hard-wired mapping.

An AI model is far more flexible. You don't have to press a single, precisely defined button. You can vary the input. Ask the model for a pasta recipe, and it gives you a recipe. Ask it to summarize a book, and it gives you a summary. The model recognizes the pattern behind your different words and adjusts its internal processing to produce a fitting answer to your specific question. So it isn't tied to one rigid input — it can handle an endless variety of texts.

That's why understanding input and output is the most important first step. Before we dig deeper into the technology, we need to be clear about one thing: every interaction with an AI is, at its core, nothing more than this transformation. You enter a character, a text, or a number. The system processes that signal using the patterns it has learned. And you get back a new character or a new text. Everything AI systems can do – from translating a language to generating images – ultimately comes down to this one basic motion: from input to result.

## What goes in and what comes out?

You type a question into a language assistant app and get an answer back. Or you upload a photo from your phone's camera and the device automatically recognizes whether there's a person, an animal, or an object in the picture. In both cases, something very similar is happening in the background: the system takes something in, processes it, and returns something else. These three steps are the basic framework behind almost every artificial intelligence.

First there's what goes in. Experts call this the input. It's everything the system receives as raw material. That can be a text, a number, an image, or even the sound of your voice. What matters here: the system doesn't see it the way you or I do. When you type the word "dog," the computer first turns those letters into a series of numbers, because internally computers can only calculate with mathematical values and don't "understand" letters. Without this first step – taking in the input and converting it – the system couldn't process anything further, because it doesn't understand human language.

The input has been converted. Now the real work begins. Because the system now has these numbers, the actual processing work starts. This is where the heart of artificial intelligence lies. The system applies the rules and patterns stored within it to extract meaning from the input. If, for example, you upload a photo of a pet, the algorithm (that's the precise computational plan, or the step-by-step instructions the system follows) checks which shapes, colors, and structures it finds and compares them with what it has learned. It matches what it sees, makes decisions, and adjusts values. This part is often the most complex, but as a user you never see it – it stays hidden in the background. All you need to know is that this is where the actual "thinking" happens.

The last step: the output. This is the output, meaning what comes out. The system translates its internal calculations back into a form you can understand. The many numbers turn back into a text, an answer to your question, or a caption under the photo. Sometimes the output is also an action, such as an autopilot turning the steering wheel slightly to the left.

The underlying principle can be boiled down to a single, central idea: an artificial intelligence is essentially a machine that maps inputs to outputs. It takes A, computes with it, and delivers B. It sounds simple, but it's the key to understanding everything. Every major AI application, whether a chatbot, an image generator, or a language translator, follows exactly this pattern. It's like a toolbox: you put various tools (the algorithms) into it, feed in raw material (input), and get a finished product (output).

Maybe you've already suspected that this process can't always be perfect? That's a very good guess. Because the system is based purely on patterns and probabilities, it can produce wrong outputs from unclear inputs. It doesn't "know" what's correct — it estimates what's most likely to be correct. That's why it's important to question the output critically. The system doesn't give you the absolute truth, but its best guess based on what it received from you. If you change the input or phrase it more clearly, the result often changes too. That way you actively steer the process and understand that input and output are closely linked.

## Diverse results: more than just text

You ask an AI for a lemon cake recipe. In the simplest case, it gives you the text of the recipe. That's familiar. But what happens if instead you say: "Also create a shopping list with quantities for four people, draw the tart as an illustration, and calculate the calories per slice"? The answer is then no longer just a block of text. It's a mosaic of different data types.

This is where you see how flexible modern systems can be. The output can be text, as just described. But it can also deliver structured data — numbers and tables that can be plugged directly into a program or a form. That matters because it means you don't have to type the calorie values in by hand; they can be sent straight into your nutrition app.

But not everything an AI produces looks like letters. Sometimes the result is an image. You have a picture of your lemon tart generated so you can post it on Instagram. Or you ask the AI to render the tart in a particular style, say a soft, light-filled style reminiscent of old paintings. The system then produces a visual file.

Another important output type is the audio file. You have the AI read the recipe aloud to you, maybe even in different voices or accents. The result is a sound file you can listen to while you cook.

The form the result takes depends on your question. An AI function can be built to take various starting points and produce different kinds of results. Ask a question, and text comes back. Enter an image description, and an image comes back. Provide numbers, and calculated values or charts come back.

This variety has a practical reason. In the real world, we rarely use information in just one form. We read instructions, look at sketches, and listen to explanations. An AI that could only spit out text would be like a cook who can only talk but isn't allowed to cook or draw. By being able to deliver different output forms, the system becomes far more useful. It can adapt to context.

Of course, there are limits. An AI can't generate a real video with motion that you can play back directly, at least not in the same way a film studio can. But it can create the individual images of a video one by one (these individual images are called frames). It also can't manufacture physical objects. But it can write the code for the 3D-printing file that produces the object on the printer.

Output isn't limited to text. It can be text, images, sounds, structured numbers, or even code. The form of the result follows the nature of the question. That makes AI systems versatile tools that fit into many areas of everyday life, from office work to weekend hobbies. You decide which form you need, and the AI delivers it.
