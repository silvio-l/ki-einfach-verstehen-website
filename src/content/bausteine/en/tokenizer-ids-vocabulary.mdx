Before you read on, split this word into pieces in your head: **“unbelievable.”** One piece, three parts such as “un–believ–able,” or every letter on its own? All three could serve as input for a computer, but they lead to very different lists of numbers. This is exactly the decision a **[tokenizer](/en/glossary/tokenizer)** makes.

In the previous lesson, the **[input](/en/glossary/input)** of a **[language model](/en/glossary/language-model)** was still a sequence of “text pieces.” Now you see what lies between your readable sentence and that input: the text is cut into pieces, and every piece gets a number. Only this sequence of whole numbers goes into the **[model](/en/glossary/model)**.

## A model never gets to see text

Your screen might show “The cats sit.” To you, that is words, spaces, and a period. A computer can store the characters as numeric codes, but that does not settle which units a language model processes. The model needs a **finite** selection of clearly separated text pieces, a list with a fixed end. You know why from the previous lesson: a language model outputs a score for every text piece it knows. Without a fixed list, it would not know how many scores to output or which belongs to what. For each piece, the model later learns its own numbers. Open-ended text must therefore first be translated into entries from a limited list.

That sounds like a side issue. Yet the split decides how long the input is for the model: one visible word can become one, two, or many pieces. They are called **[tokens](/en/glossary/token)**. How text is split is fixed by the tokenizer before the model calculates anything.

The tokenizer does not understand the sentence or pick pieces by feel. It runs a fixed encoding procedure. The same text and the same tokenizer normally produce the same token sequence; a different tokenizer may split it differently. So there is no *one natural tokenization* hidden inside language.

## Why not simply number every word?

The most obvious idea: take a dictionary, give every word a number, done. “The” could be 417, “cats” 982, and “sit” 771. For a carefully limited collection of texts, that works. For open-ended language, it does not.

Which entries would the list need? “Friend,” “friendly,” “unfriendly,” “unfriendliness,” and every further word built the same way? Plus names, product labels, dialect, typos, forms such as “learn,” “learns,” and “learned,” web addresses, and words coined tomorrow. A list can grow large, but it stays limited, while language keeps forming new character sequences.

Unknown words could share a single placeholder. But then two completely different new names or technical terms would both shrink to the same “unknown” symbol. Their letters, which often offer clues, would be gone. A chatbot would see nothing but “unknown” for every new name, say that of a band founded last week.

A pure word list also wastes knowledge: four separate IDs for “learn,” “learns,” “learned,” and “learner” hide that they share the same characters.

## Why not take every letter on its own?

At the other end lies an equally simple solution: every letter and punctuation mark becomes a token. Then almost any word can be assembled, even a new one, and the list of pieces stays small. “Cats,” however, now takes four steps instead of perhaps one or two.

In a long document, this multiplies the positions the model has to process. With spaces and the period, “The cats sit.” already has 13 character positions; a coarser split needs far fewer tokens. Shorter sequences mean fewer positions and so less computation for the model. A chatbot would need a separate round of the text loop for every letter of its answer.

Single characters also carry very little. The model would have to rebuild frequent sequences such as “ing”, “tion”, or “str” from many positions again and again. Whole words are too coarse and rigid; single characters are flexible but needlessly fine-grained. A workable middle ground is needed.

## The useful middle ground: reusable word pieces

<Figure kind="illustration" caption="Like a building set: frequent things come as large finished pieces, rare things you assemble from small parts. Unlike a building set, nobody designed the pieces; they were learned from text.">
<img src="/bausteine/tokenizer-ids-vokabular/baukasten.png" alt="A wooden building set on a table: in front, a long row of a few large blocks, filled up at the end with small blocks; behind it, a box with sorted large blocks and many small ones" width="800" height="694" />
</Figure>

Many modern text tokenizers therefore use **[subword tokens](/en/glossary/subword-token)**: a token can be a frequent whole word, a recurring word part, or a single character. The tokenizers behind well-known chatbots such as ChatGPT work this way, too. Frequent patterns get compact entries; rare sequences are assembled from smaller known parts. “Learning,” for example, might split into “learn” and “ing.” Only a thought example: real tokenizers often know such a common word whole, or might choose “le”, “arn”, “ing”. Which pieces exist depends on the text material and the procedure that built the vocabulary. And “unbelievable” from the start? A subword tokenizer might keep it whole or take two or three frequent pieces, chosen by its learned vocabulary, not by syllable rules.

Some tokenizers go all the way down to bytes. A **byte** is a small numeric unit in computer memory; every visible character is stored as one or more bytes, an accented letter or an emoji as several. Such a tokenizer needs no “unknown” placeholder: even a never-seen name can be assembled from bytes, and its letters are preserved.

This middle ground is not perfect. A frequent pattern is represented briefly; an unusual spelling can fall apart into many small pieces. So the token count is not the word count, and two sentences of similar length can need different numbers of tokens. Even capitalization, spaces, or an accent can change the split.

Where do the pieces come from? A widely used procedure called [BPE](/en/glossary/byte-pair-encoding) learns them from text by gradually merging frequent pairs of characters into larger pieces.

## Keeping token, tokenizer, and vocabulary apart

How does the tokenizer know which pieces exist, such as “ cat” or “s”? Three things are easy to confuse here. A **token** is a single unit, for example “ cat” (with a space in front, more on that shortly), “s”, or “.”. The **[vocabulary](/en/glossary/vocabulary)** is the complete, finite mapping of all permitted units to IDs. The **[tokenizer](/en/glossary/tokenizer)** is the procedure, including rules and vocabulary, that splits text into these units and joins them back into text.

Picture the vocabulary as a card index: each card holds a text piece and an identifying number. The tokenizer finds a matching sequence of cards for your sentence and outputs their numbers. On the way back, it looks up the numbers and joins the pieces again. Both directions run with every chatbot message: there with your question, back with every piece of the answer.

The picture has a limit: the tokenizer does not search for cards that “fit the meaning.” Its splitting rules determine unambiguously which sequence is chosen. And the card with “ cat” holds no definition of a cat, only a sequence of characters. What the model later connects with this position emerges only through its trained **[parameters](/en/glossary/parameters)**.

## An ID is a label, not a meaning

Each text piece in the vocabulary has a whole number, its **[token ID](/en/glossary/token-id)**. Suppose “The” carries ID 417. This number is not the word translated into mathematics, and it measures neither meaning nor frequency, mood, or importance. It is just the number of an entry — like the number on a locker at the swimming pool. The 417 does not tell you what is inside; only the fixed link between number and locker does. So when a chatbot continues “The” sensibly, that is not because of the 417 but because of what its model has learned about it.

<Figure kind="icon" caption="An ID is only a label – like the number on a locker, not the contents themselves.">
<img src="/bausteine/tokenizer-ids-vokabular/etikett.svg" alt="A label tag" width="240" height="240" />
</Figure>

That is why a token ID is ambiguous without its tokenizer. In another vocabulary, 417 can stand for “and,” part of a word, or a special character. Even if two tokenizers know the same text pieces, their numbers need not match. Calculating with IDs as quantities would be pointless too: ID 417 plus ID 82 does not give you the meaning of ID 499.

The model uses these numbers as lookup addresses: every permitted ID will later have its own list of learned numbers. How such lists are organized, and how an ID selects its list from a large table, is the topic of the next lesson.

## A complete toy example

Here is an invented mini vocabulary. It only makes the process visible; a real tokenizer splits differently.

| Token ID | Text piece |
| ---: | :--- |
| 417 | “The” |
| 82 | “ cat” |
| 903 | “s” |
| 771 | “ sit” |
| 13 | “.” |

During **[encoding](/en/glossary/tokenizer)**, the tokenizer receives the text “The cats sit.” With this mini vocabulary, there is exactly one split: “The” + “ cat” + “s” + “ sit” + “.”, because “ cats” is not in the list. Real tokenizers also split by fixed rules. Looking the pieces up in the vocabulary gives the sequence 417, 82, 903, 771, 13. These five numbers are the model’s input.

During **[decoding](/en/glossary/tokenizer)**, the mapping runs backwards: the tokenizer looks up each ID and joins the five stored pieces in the same order. Because two tokens already carry their leading space, the result is “The cats sit.” again. An extra space would change it.

The example also shows why order matters. 417, 82, 903 is “The cats”; 82, 903, 417 gives “ catsThe”. The IDs are not an unordered collection but a sequence in which every position is kept. From such sequences, a language model later learns which token is likely to come next.

## Spaces and punctuation are part of the input

People often treat spaces as empty gaps. For a tokenizer, they are characters like any other. Some vocabularies store frequent words with a leading space as separate entries. So “cat” with no space in front can be split differently from “ cat” in the middle of a sentence. Line breaks, tabs, quotation marks, and punctuation can likewise be tokens or part of larger units. Paste a table with lots of blank lines into a chatbot, and those characters become tokens, too.

That is why rules of thumb such as “one token is about four characters” only give you a rough idea.

## Why the tokenizer and the model form a fixed pair

The model was trained with exactly one mapping. For input 417, its parameters draw on the representation trained for entry 417. Swap only the tokenizer so that 417 means “and” instead of “The,” and the model receives valid numbers but the wrong symbols. It is like reading a book with a mixed-up index: the numbers point to the wrong contents.

<Figure kind="icon" caption="A mismatched tokenizer reads like a book with a mixed-up index – the numbers point to the wrong pages.">
<img src="/bausteine/tokenizer-ids-vokabular/buch-verzeichnis.svg" alt="An open book" width="240" height="240" />

The vocabulary size has to match as well. Otherwise a tokenizer could produce an ID for which the model has no entry, or entries in the model’s lookup table could stay unused because the tokenizer never produces their IDs. So tokenizer files, vocabulary, rules, and special tokens are shipped and versioned together with a model. That is why a new chatbot model often comes with its own tokenizer, and the same sentence gives different IDs there.

Special entries include markers for structure, such as the beginning and end of a sequence, or reserved control information. They do not look like words, but for the model they are numbered entries, too. Their meaning is defined by the model package; no special ID does the same thing everywhere.

<details>
<summary>One level deeper: how BPE learns its vocabulary</summary>

**Byte Pair Encoding**, or **[BPE](/en/glossary/byte-pair-encoding)**, began as a data compression method. For machine translation, it was adapted to represent rare and unknown words as sequences of smaller units instead of discarding them. BPE starts with small digital units. Depending on the variant, these are characters or the bytes explained earlier; the name stems from the original compression method, which used bytes. It counts which neighboring pairs occur most often in the training material and merges the most frequent pair into a new unit; that is the “Pair Encoding.” This repeats until the desired vocabulary size or number of merges is reached.

If “l e a r n”, “l e a r n s”, and “l e a r n e d” are frequent in the material, letter pairs might become units first, and later larger sequences such as “learn.” Rare endings remain composable from smaller parts. The actual result depends on the material and the rules; the example only shows the mechanism.

In use, the tokenizer replays the learned merges on new text in the same order, so the same text always gives the same split. BPE is not the only subword method. **[SentencePiece](/en/glossary/sentencepiece)** learns subword models, including BPE, directly from unmodified sentences, without first cutting them at presumed word boundaries. This helps with languages that do not mark word boundaries with spaces the way English does. The shared idea: a limited set of learned units should represent any input as usefully as possible.

</details>

## Why you notice tokens in everyday use

In a long chat with an AI assistant, the model at some point seems to forget what was said at the beginning. Or a service reports that your text is too long, although it is only a few pages. In both cases, the issue is not words but tokens.

Models process only a limited number of token positions at once, because memory and computation are limited. Depending on the system, this **[context window](/en/glossary/context-window)** covers the input and the generated **[output](/en/glossary/output)**. You know from the previous lesson why it forgets: in a chat, the whole conversation so far goes back into the model in every round, and it grows with each answer. Once it no longer fits, the system must drop, shorten, or split part of it.

Some texts fall apart into especially many small tokens: an unusual product code, a long string of digits, or a language the vocabulary covers less compactly. They use more positions than familiar text of the same length, and many providers bill per token. For a concrete limit or cost estimate, count with the provider’s counting tool, not with a character formula. More tokens also mean more computing work.

## What the tokenizer does not do

The tokenizer recognizes neither word meanings nor grammar nor intention. Sometimes its boundaries look linguistically sensible — such as “learn” and “ing.” That can be because these sequences were frequent in the training material; it is not a linguistic analysis. A token may cut right through a syllable, an ending, or a name.

Nor does the tokenizer decide which token comes next. It converts existing text into IDs and generated IDs back into text. Prediction happens in the model. Nor does the vocabulary store definitions, only the permitted symbols and their numbers. Learned behavior lives in the model parameters, not in a secret dictionary entry next to each ID.

A common misconception: a model “understands” a word if it is a single token; if the word falls apart into many tokens, the model understands it less well or not at all. That sounds plausible, because one compact unit seems more complete than several fragments.

In fact, the model learned patterns across whole token sequences during training and can combine information from several positions. Whether a word is one token or several describes only its technical representation. So a token count or a visually “neat” split tells you about the length of the representation, not about whether the model grasps the content. Still, the split has consequences: for some tasks, such as arithmetic with multi-digit numbers, performance measurably depends on how the digits are divided into tokens.

## The numbers are only the beginning

The complete path so far: visible text → tokenizer rules → token sequence → vocabulary lookup → sequence of token IDs. The text is numbered, but not yet in a form in which the **[model](/en/glossary/model)** can calculate similarities and relationships. A single whole number carries no direction, closeness, or learned property.

In the next step, every ID serves as the address of a long list of learned numbers. What exactly is such a list, and how do you organize many of them, one per token? That is the topic of the next lesson: scalar, **[vector](/en/glossary/vector)**, matrix, and **[tensor](/en/glossary/tensor)**.

If you remember only one sentence, make it this one: **A token is a reusable text piece, its ID is only its number in the vocabulary, and only the model turns that number into a learned representation.**
